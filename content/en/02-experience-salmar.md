# SalMar: understanding industry from the ground up

**Type:** 3rd-year internship (paid summer job)
**Company:** SalMar AS, InnovaMar site, island of Frøya (Norway), Post-Rigor department
**Dates:** 11 May to 21 August 2026 (15 weeks)
**Successive roles:** production worker (weeks 1 to 12) → Deep Tracker line operator (weeks 13 and 14) → Palletring robotic cell operator (week 15)
**Company supervisor:** Marisa Ribeiro

## The context

SalMar is the world's 2nd-largest producer of farmed salmon. Its InnovaMar site, on an island of 2,000 inhabitants more than three hours' drive from Trondheim, runs 24/7. Automation is everywhere: sensor-paced conveyors, precision graders, industrial controllers, palletising robots. Employees of 52 nationalities work side by side.

## Why this choice

I am heading towards AI, not the food industry. Yet I deliberately chose a production worker position, for two reasons. The first is a conviction: you cannot become a good manager, or a good designer of automated systems, without having lived what the people working alongside the machines live. The second is a personal challenge: testing my physical and mental limits in a demanding, isolated environment, in a language I did not speak.

## What I did

**Weeks 1 to 12 · Production worker.** Cutting, pin-bone removal, quality control, vacuum packing, palletising, at the pace of one fillet every two seconds, on 8 to 12-hour shifts alternating mornings and evenings, in the cold. I discovered what a bottleneck concretely is: a single slowdown immediately ripples through the whole line.

**Weeks 13 and 14 · Deep Tracker line operator.** Promoted after two months (against one to two years on average on the site), I took responsibility for the packaging line and its 22 operators. I split the team according to production, prioritised the product references, handled restocking and production changeovers. I also carried out first-level maintenance on the line's 6 machines: reading error codes on the HMIs, adjusting variables to hold the pace.

**Week 15 · Palletring robotic cell operator.** I operated two ABB IRB 660 palletising robots and supervised 3 people. My job: adjusting target coordinates on the FlexPendant and occasionally correcting RAPID routines when a crate arrived badly positioned. It was the direct application of my robotics courses in Finland, but with real, variable loads at high speed.

## The situation that sums up this internship

An automated trimming machine (Marel FleXicut), which combines X-rays, 3D vision and water-jet cutting, broke down. I asked to follow the technicians throughout the repair day. By asking them how the machine worked, I noticed a recurring defect: at the exit, fillets overlapped, and one person was tied up full-time realigning them. The mechanical rails already tried were damaging the flesh.

I suggested a software solution rather than a mechanical one: modify the controller's program to create a slight speed difference on the receiving belt, so that the fillets would separate by themselves. The idea was tested in real conditions over a full shift. The principle worked, but the conveyor's motors were not powerful enough to separate the most stuck-together fillets. A real solution would also require changing the motorisation. The idea nonetheless restarted the maintenance team's thinking about an alternative to manual correction.

## The difficulties

- **Physical:** pace, cold, damp, alternating hours.
- **Managerial:** giving unambiguous instructions to people of different cultures and languages. A misunderstood instruction can cost as much as a breakdown.
- **Technical:** working on existing machines and processes, with no room to redefine them, which forces you to understand their logic in depth.

## What I take from it

| Skill | How I developed it |
|---|---|
| Team management | 22 people supervised, assigned and prioritised in real time |
| Intercultural communication | Adapting my English and my tone to each person, and checking that an instruction is understood rather than assuming it |
| Industrial robotics | RAPID programming, FlexPendant, ABB IRB 660 cell |
| Industrial systems | Controllers, sensors, HMIs, reading error codes |
| Applied computer vision | Analysing how the FleXicut works (X-ray and 3D vision fusion) and its limits |
| Problem solving | Diagnosing the exit defect and proposing a tested solution |
| Resilience | Holding the pace and the standard for 15 weeks |

**The result I am proudest of:** my leader, who had taken the risk of promoting me, invited me to come back the following year as assistant leader, the second most important position in the factory.

## The link with my target role

This internship, conceived as a break far from computing, put me face to face with one of the most concrete uses of industrial AI: a machine that decides in under a second how to cut each fillet. I also saw the limits of computer vision on a product as variable as a fish fillet, and the place that remains for the human eye. This is exactly where I want to work: where AI meets the physical world.
